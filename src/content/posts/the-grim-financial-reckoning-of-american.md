American higher education's long-deferred financial reckoning has arrived. More than one fourth of private colleges are now in danger of closure. Postsecondary enrollment as a whole has dropped by 2.3 million students since 2010, and new international student visa issuances suffered a 36% collapse in just one year. The 56.3% tuition discount rate for first-time full-time undergraduates in 2024–25 stands at an all-time record, which means the sticker price on which the sector's business model depends has become largely fictional.

The familiar scapegoat — faculty salaries — is the wrong target. Between 1993 and 2021, full-time non-instructional staff at American degree-granting institutions grew by 93%, while full-time faculty grew by only 53%. Administrative expansion, not teaching, drove the cost curve. Indiscriminate budget cutting deepens the hole: eliminating programs raises per-student costs and depresses enrollment further, accelerating the spiral it was meant to arrest.

The way forward is not a forced choice between the residential campus and online delivery. It requires reimagining the university across three modalities: traditional in-person instruction, asynchronous coursework, and — most neglected — synchronous online teaching. Evidence from the California Community Colleges and Carnegie Mellon's Open Learning Initiative indicates synchronous instruction can match classroom outcomes at materially lower cost. Artificial intelligence, deployed alongside human faculty rather than in place of them, widens that reach.

Institutions also misread their students. "What Gen Z does value is flexibility, career relevance, and appreciable interaction that is not merely transactional or expressive."

The iconic residential college will endure for those who can afford it. It will no longer be the norm.
